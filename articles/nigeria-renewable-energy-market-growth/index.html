Nigeria's Renewable Market Is Growing ~26% a Year — Where the Real Money Is

The growth numbers are real. So is the hype around some of them. Here's an honest breakdown of where the money is actually flowing — and where it isn't yet.

The Actual Numbers

Multiple market research firms have published Nigeria renewable energy forecasts recently, and while the exact figures differ, the direction is consistent: fast growth from a small base. Installed renewable capacity was estimated at roughly 3.6–3.7 GW in 2025, with projections putting it near 14 GW by 2031 — a compound annual growth rate in the mid-to-high 20% range, close to the 26% figure now circulating. Extrapolated to 2030, that trajectory lands somewhere around 11–12 GW, broadly consistent with the number driving this conversation.

Worth flagging upfront: different research firms show meaningfully different baselines — some put 2024's capacity at 3.1 GW, others put 2026 alone at 4.5 GW. Treat the growth rate as directionally reliable and the precise GW figures as estimates, not settled facts.

Some of the underlying activity behind these numbers is concrete, not projected. Nigeria added roughly 803 MW of solar capacity in 2025 alone — a 141% year-on-year jump. Commercial and industrial (C&I) demand for renewables is forecast to grow even faster than the overall market, at roughly 30% CAGR through 2031.

Who's Already Winning

Hydropower still dominates Nigeria's existing renewable mix, holding an estimated 87% share of installed renewable capacity in 2025. That's largely legacy infrastructure, not new investment activity — big hydro projects are capital-intensive, slow to build, and not where new entrants are putting money today.

The companies actually active in new project development and financing include a mix of international majors and Nigeria-focused specialists: TotalEnergies, Engie, Sinohydro and JinkoSolar on the international/manufacturing side, alongside Nigerian and Africa-focused players like Starsight Energy, North South Power, and mini-grid operator Husk Power Systems. Utilities still hold the largest share of the market by end-user segment, but that share is shrinking relative to the C&I segment's growth rate.

Where the Real Money Is

Strip away the headline GW figures and a few specific areas stand out as where capital is actually moving right now:

1. Commercial & Industrial Solar Hybrids

C&I demand is growing faster than the market overall for a simple reason: grid unreliability makes diesel backup expensive and predictable-cost solar attractive to any factory, mill, or facility with real load. Nigeria's 2025 National Industrial Policy explicitly mandates industrial clusters adopt solar hybrids and backs Independent Power Projects within manufacturing zones — a direct policy tailwind behind this segment.

2. Mini-Grid and Off-Grid Financing

With close to half the population lacking reliable grid access, distributed and off-grid solar remains a large, underserved market. This is also where local-currency debt financing has started showing up at meaningful scale — United Capital's naira-denominated revolving facility to Husk Power Systems is one concrete example of domestic capital, not just foreign aid or grants, backing mini-grid operators.

3. Local Manufacturing and Assembly

Policy incentives are pushing toward local assembly of solar equipment rather than pure importation. For investors, this is a longer-horizon play tied to industrial policy support rather than a quick-return opportunity — but it's one of the few segments with real government backing behind it.

4. Long-Term Power Purchase Agreements (PPAs)

Reforms under the Electricity Act 2023 have decentralized regulatory oversight to the state level, allowing states to set their own feed-in tariffs. Some PPAs in the market now run 20 years at fixed tariffs below $0.15/kWh — attractive cost predictability for buyers, and a bankable structure for financiers, assuming forex and policy risk are properly priced in.

Where the Hype Outruns the Substance

Not every eye-catching statistic in this market means what it sounds like:

  • Wind's "87% CAGR." Some reports project wind installations growing at an extraordinary compound rate through 2031. That number is real, but it's a percentage of a tiny existing base — Nigeria's installed wind capacity today is minimal. A huge growth rate off a near-zero starting point is not the same as a large addressable opportunity yet.
  • Utility-scale solar without grid solutions attached. Nigeria's aging, capacity-constrained grid remains a genuine bottleneck. Utility-scale projects that depend purely on grid integration — rather than distributed, off-grid, or hybrid models — face a harder, slower path than the headline growth numbers suggest.
  • Treating every market report's GW figure as gospel. As shown above, credible firms differ by billions of dollars and multiple gigawatts on baseline figures for the same year. Anyone pitching an investment case built on one single, precise number from one report is oversimplifying a genuinely uncertain data landscape.

The Policy Tailwinds Behind the Growth

This isn't growth happening in a policy vacuum. A few concrete developments are doing real work behind the scenes:

  • The Electricity Act 2023 decentralized power sector oversight to state governments, letting states set feed-in tariffs suited to local resources instead of a single national framework.
  • The Renewable Energy Master Plan targets raising renewables' share of installed generation capacity from 13% in 2015 to roughly 23–25% by 2025 and 30–36% by 2030.
  • Seven-year tax holidays and feed-in tariffs are structured to make renewables cost-competitive with diesel and gas once currency risk is factored in.

What Could Slow This Down

Being real about the upside means being equally real about the risks:

  • Grid infrastructure remains aging and capacity-constrained, which limits how much utility-scale renewable power can actually reach end users even once built
  • Currency and forex access continue to affect project bankability, especially for anything with imported equipment or foreign-denominated financing
  • Regulatory uncertainty at the state level, following decentralization, means feed-in tariff terms and enforcement can vary significantly by jurisdiction
  • Forecast divergence itself — when research firms disagree this much on baseline numbers, it signals a market that's still maturing its own data infrastructure, which is itself a risk factor for underwriting

Our Verdict

📈

The growth is real. The best opportunities are narrower than the headline number suggests.

C&I solar, mini-grid debt financing, and local manufacturing tied to industrial policy are where capital is actually moving today. Pure utility-scale plays and thinly-based percentage growth stats deserve more scrutiny than the press releases give them.

Conclusion

Nigeria's renewable energy market is genuinely one of the fastest-growing in Africa right now, and the underlying drivers — grid unreliability, policy reform, falling technology costs — are structural, not temporary. But "26% annual growth" as a single headline number flattens a market where hydropower dominance, thin-base percentage stats, and grid bottlenecks all coexist with real, fundable opportunity.
